What these things are, and why they're worth claiming

WebGL is the browser's API to the graphics card. Three.js is a library on top of WebGL — it gives you cameras, meshes and materials so you never have to talk to the GPU directly. GLSL is the language of programs that run on the GPU, and there are two kinds: a vertex shader runs once per point and decides where it lands on screen; a fragment shader runs once per pixel and decides what colour it is. Both run on thousands of cores simultaneously, which is the whole point.

The résumé distinction that matters: "used Three.js" and "wrote GLSL" are not the same claim. Most computational designers can honestly say the first. Far fewer can say the second, and it's checkable — which is exactly why it's worth saying when it's true.

Where it sits in the discipline. Three reasons it's more than decoration:

1. It's the only way past a scale ceiling. A JavaScript loop over agents or data points dies somewhere around 10⁴. Your High Line pheromone field and the Titan catalogue both cross that line by moving the work to the GPU. For a designer working at city scale — every tax lot, every street frame, every LiDAR return — this is the difference between a study that runs and one that doesn't.
2. It's parametric thinking in a different execution model. A shader is a pure function evaluated in parallel across a domain. If you can think in Grasshopper, you already have the mental model; what's new is that there are no loops and no shared state. That transfer is genuinely easier coming from your background than from a general web background, and it's worth knowing that.
3. It's what separates "designer who makes diagrams" from "designer who builds instruments." Your billow.frag decision — that hover resolves a card rather than highlighting it, so attention is the only thing producing saturation — is a design argument implemented in a fragment shader. That's the job.

Where it's hiring. Design-technology groups building custom viewers (SOM, Gensler, Zaha CODE, Thornton Tomasetti's CORE), real-time/immersive studios, scientific and data visualization teams, and installation/media practices. In those postings it usually appears as "WebGL/Three.js," "shader authoring," or "real-time graphics" — and the ones that say shader specifically are filtering for exactly the smaller group.
